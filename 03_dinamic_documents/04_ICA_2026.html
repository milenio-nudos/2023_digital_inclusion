Methods

Study design

This study draws on data from the National Digital Inclusion Survey conducted in Chile between August and October 2023, encompassing both rural and urban localities across the country. The survey was administered face-to-face by trained interviewers from a professional polling firm (Feedback).

The sample is probabilistic and representative of individuals aged 14 and older who are permanent residents of the selected locations. In urban areas, a stratified cluster sampling strategy was employed, with random selection of households within predefined blocks. In rural areas—characterized by smaller, dispersed populations—a random-route method was used, approximating a near-census coverage in each locality. One respondent per household was randomly selected for interview. The final analytic sample consists of 1,200 respondents, of whom 751 (62.6%) reside in urban areas and 449 (37.4%) in rural communities. The overall response rate (RR4), calculated following the American Association for Public Opinion Research guidelines, was 47.5%.

Measures

Algorithmic Awareness

The dependent variable follows the Algorithmic Media Content Awareness (AMCA) scale (Zarouali et al., 2021), capturing respondents’ awareness that algorithms curate online content. It includes three items measuring: content filtering, behavioral dependence, and ethical implications (e.g., “Algorithms use my personal data, which has consequences for my online privacy”). Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all aware to 5 = Completely aware) and averaged into a single scale (α > .80). Higher scores indicate greater algorithmic awareness.

Digital skills

Digital skills were measured following van Deursen’s (CITA) multidimensional framework, encompassing four subdimensions derived from items in battery C3:

  • Operational skills — ability to manage basic technological operations such as installing apps or adjusting device settings [6 items].

  • Informational skills — capacity to search for, evaluate, and organize online information [5 items].

  • Social skills — ability to communicate, interact, and manage privacy on social media [6 items].

  • Creative skills — ability to create, edit, and share original digital content [3 items].

Each scales are sumative from dichotomical items phrased as know how to do (1). The scales have different number of items, so to conserve comparability, they were standardized (0–1). Higher values denote stronger digital competencies in each domain.

Social media and platforms use

Social media use was measured through a battery of six items capturing the frequency of use of major platforms: Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, WhatsApp, TikTok, X (Twitter) and Netflix. Responses ranged from 1 (Never or less than once a month) to 5 (Several times a day).

Sociodemographic Controls

Control variables included are age (continuos, in years), gender (0 = male; 1 = female), educational status (1 = primary or less, to 5 = college or more), household income (9 values ordinal scale with ranges adapted to number of members) and the type of residence, coded as urban or rural following the national Statistics Institute’s classification.

Analytical strategy

We estimated a series of linear regression models to examine the predictors of algorithmic awareness. The main independent variables were the four dimensions of digital skills and the six social media use indicators, controlling for demographical variables. All continuous variables were mean-centered prior to analysis to facilitate interpretation. Robust standard errors were computed to account for potential heteroskedasticity.

Model diagnostics confirmed the absence of multicollinearity (all VIF < 2.5) and normal distribution of residuals. Analyses were conducted using R 4.4.2 software. Marginal effects and standardized coefficients are reported for comparability. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Linear Models

Three nested models were then specified to assess the explanatory power of demographic factors, digital skills, and social media platform use. Diagnostic tests were performed to ensure the validity of the model, revealing that none of the variables exhibited a Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) greater than 5. This result is favorable, as it indicates that multicollinearity is not a concern, thereby strengthening the reliability and stability of the coefficient estimates.

The evaluation of the models’ goodness-of-fit indicates that Model 2 offers the most parsimonious and robust explanation for the variance in algorithmic awareness. This model, which incorporates both demographic variables and digital skills, presents the highest adjusted R² value (R² adj. = .547) and the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC = 161.033). The inclusion of social media usage variables in Model 3 did not improve the model’s fit; rather, it weakened it slightly and reduced the number of observations.

The analysis of the coefficients from the superior Model 2 reveals the preponderant influence of digital skills as the most significant set of predictors. Specifically, operational skills (β = 0.31, p<.001), creative skills (β = 0.16, p<.001), and informative skills (β = 0.12, p<.05) are all positively and significantly associated with greater algorithmic awareness. Regarding demographic variables, the effect of educational level, which was significant in Model 1 (β = 0.04, p<.001), becomes non-significant in Model 2 (β = -0.00, n.s.) after the introduction of digital skills. This result suggests that the impact of formal education on algorithmic awareness is mediated by the development of digital competencies. Furthermore, household income (β = 0.03, p<.01) and residence in urban areas (β = -0.06, p<.01) maintain a positive and significant association. Age presents a small but significant negative relationship (β = -0.00, p<.001).

Although Model 3 demonstrated a weaker fit, its coefficients offer relevant nuances regarding platform use. The use of Twitter (β = 0.04, p<.001) and Netflix (β = 0.04, p<.001) is positively associated with algorithmic awareness, whereas the use of Facebook correlates negatively (β = -0.02, p<.05). The use of other platforms such as YouTube, Instagram, or TikTok yielded no statistically significant effects. In conclusion, the findings demonstrate that digital skills are the central predictor of algorithmic awareness, surpassing the importance of traditional sociodemographic factors and the usage patterns of specific social platforms.
