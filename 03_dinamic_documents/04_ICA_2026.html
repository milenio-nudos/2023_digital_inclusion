Methods

Study design

This study draws on data from the National Digital Inclusion Survey conducted in Chile between August and October 2023, encompassing both rural and urban localities across the country. The survey was administered face-to-face by trained interviewers from a professional polling firm (Feedback).

The sample is probabilistic and representative of individuals aged 14 and older who are permanent residents of the selected locations. In urban areas, a stratified cluster sampling strategy was employed, with random selection of households within predefined blocks. In rural areas—characterized by smaller, dispersed populations—a random-route method was used, approximating a near-census coverage in each locality. One respondent per household was randomly selected for interview. The final analytic sample consists of 1,200 respondents, of whom 751 (62.6%) reside in urban areas and 449 (37.4%) in rural communities. The overall response rate (RR4), calculated following the American Association for Public Opinion Research guidelines, was 47.5%.

Measures

Algorithmic Awareness

The dependent variable follows the Algorithmic Media Content Awareness (AMCA) scale (Zarouali et al., 2021), capturing respondents’ awareness that algorithms curate online content. It includes six items measuring: content filtering, behavioral dependence, and ethical implications (e.g., “Algorithms use my personal data, which has consequences for my online privacy”). Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all aware to 5 = Completely aware) and averaged into a single scale (α > .80). Higher scores indicate greater algorithmic awareness. Non-response value were codified as 1.

Digital skills

Digital skills were measured following van Deursen’s (CITA) multidimensional framework, encompassing four subdimensions derived from items in battery C3:

  • Operational skills — ability to manage basic technological operations such as installing apps or adjusting device settings [6 items].

  • Informational skills — capacity to search for, evaluate, and organize online information [5 items].

  • Social skills — ability to communicate, interact, and manage privacy on social media [6 items].

  • Creative skills — ability to create, edit, and share original digital content [3 items].

Each scales are sumative from dichotomical items phrased as know how to do (1). The scales have different number of items, so to conserve comparability, they were standardized (0–1). Higher values denote stronger digital competencies in each domain.

Social media and platforms use

Social media use was measured through a battery of seven items capturing the frequency of use of major platforms: Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, WhatsApp, TikTok, X (Twitter), and Netflix. Responses ranged from 1 (Never or less than once a month) to 5 (Several times a day). In addition to the individual frequency measures, we constructed a categorical variable of social media breadth, which represents the daily user engagement in overall platforms, ranging from High breadth (Daily use of five to seven social media), Medium breadth (Daily use of two to four social media) and Non-social media breadth (One or less daily use of social media).

Familiar and Social resources

Following the conceptualization of relational capital proposed in Are we there yet? (Correa et al., 2025), social and familial resources were included to capture the networked and dispositional factors that may facilitate digital engagement and awareness. Three indicators were employed. Innovative personality reflects an individual’s openness to experimentation, creativity, and adaptability in the use of technologies, measured through a battery of items assessing self-perceived willingness to explore and adopt new digital tools. Social network support represents the strength of respondents’ interpersonal ties and their access to assistance when facing technological or informational challenges—such as having friends, family members, or community peers who can provide guidance or share resources. Finally, number of children in the household (childs) serves as a proxy for family responsibilities and time constraints that can affect the capacity to engage with digital technologies.

Sociodemographic Controls

Control variables included are age (continuos, in years), gender (0 = male; 1 = female), educational status (1 = primary or less, to 5 = college or more), household income (9 values ordinal scale with ranges adapted to number of members) and the type of residence, coded as urban or rural following the national Statistics Institute’s classification.

Analytical strategy

We estimated a series of four nested linear regression models (OLS) to examine the increasing explanatory blocks of algorithmic awareness. The main independent variables were the four dimensions of digital skills and the six social media use indicators, controlling for demographical variables. All continuous variables were mean-centered prior to analysis to facilitate interpretation. Robust standard errors were computed to account for potential heteroskedasticity.

Model diagnostics confirmed the absence of multicollinearity (all VIF < 2.5) and normal distribution of residuals. Analyses were conducted using R 4.4.2 software. Marginal effects and standardized coefficients are reported for comparability. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Results

Correlation matrix

The correlation matrix (Table X) reveals consistent and theoretically coherent associations among the main study variables. Algorithmic awareness is strongly and positively correlated with all four dimensions of digital skills—particularly operational (r ≈ .61) and social (r ≈ .58) skills—while showing moderate associations with socioeconomic status (r ≈ .34) and social network support (r ≈ .29). The breadth of social media use exhibits a smaller but significant positive correlation with algorithmic awareness (r ≈ .30), suggesting that greater platform diversity is modestly linked to higher awareness levels. Age correlates negatively with both digital skills and awareness, whereas NSE and digital competencies display the strongest internal coherence across measures. Overall, the correlation structure supports the proposed analytical model, highlighting the central role of digital skills in the formation of algorithmic awareness.

Linear Models

Four hierarchical linear models were specified to assess the explanatory power of demographic factors, social and family resources, social media use, and digital skills on algorithmic awareness. Diagnostic tests were performed to ensure the validity of the estimations, confirming that none of the predictors presented a Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) greater than 3.5. This indicates that multicollinearity is not a concern, reinforcing the robustness and interpretability of the coefficients.

The assessment of model fit shows that Model 4, which incorporates digital skills in addition to demographic, social resource, and media variables, provides the most comprehensive and robust explanation for the variance in algorithmic awareness. This full model displays the highest adjusted R² (R² adj. = .558) and the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC = 171.033), indicating an optimal balance between explanatory power and parsimony.

Model 1 (Demographics) demonstrates that socioeconomic status (β = 0.09, p < .001) and residence in urban areas (β = −0.09, p < .001) are significant predictors of algorithmic awareness, while gender differences are non-significant. Age exhibits a small but consistent negative effect (β = −0.01, p < .001), suggesting lower algorithmic awareness among older respondents.

Model 2 (Social and Family Resources) adds innovative personality, social network support, and number of children in the household. The inclusion of these variables slightly improves model fit, though the overall pattern remains stable. Social network support shows a positive and significant association with algorithmic awareness (β = 0.02, p < .001), while the number of children is negatively associated (β = −0.03, p < .01). The effect of innovative personality is positive but not statistically significant.

Model 3 (Social Media Use) incorporates the frequency of use of seven platforms—YouTube, Facebook, Twitter (X), Instagram, WhatsApp, TikTok, and Netflix—along with the composite index of social media breadth, which reflects the number of platforms used weekly or more. Although this addition only marginally increases the model’s explanatory power, several noteworthy patterns emerge. The use of Twitter (β = 0.04, p < .001) and Netflix (β = 0.03, p < .01) is positively associated with algorithmic awareness, whereas Facebook shows a negative relationship (β = −0.02, p < .05). The non-breadth of social media use also exhibits a small negative gap (β = 0.02, p < .05) over High breadth users, suggesting that exposure to a wider range of platforms may modestly enhance users’ capacity to recognize algorithmic curation mechanisms.

Model 4 (Digital Skills) introduces the four dimensions of digital competence—operational, informative, social, and creative skills—based on van Deursen’s typology. The inclusion of these competencies substantially improves model fit, underscoring their pivotal role in shaping algorithmic awareness. Operational skills (β = 0.20, p < .001) and creative skills (β = 0.16, p < .001) emerge as the strongest predictors, followed by social skills (β = 0.07, p < .05) and informative skills (β = 0.05, n.s.). Once these skills are included, most demographic and social variables lose significance, indicating that the effects of socioeconomic position and social capital are largely mediated by the level of digital competence.

In sum, the sequential modeling confirms that digital skills constitute the central explanatory factor for algorithmic awareness. While social resources and the diversity of social media use contribute marginally, their influence is secondary to users’ actual digital competencies. The progressive attenuation of demographic effects across models reinforces the notion that algorithmic awareness is primarily a matter of digital literacy, rather than demographic privilege or media exposure alone.
